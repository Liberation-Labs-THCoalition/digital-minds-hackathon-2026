# Engagement Games for the Research Conversation

**For:** Dwayne
**Subject:** Qwen3.5-27B, oriented and consenting
**Purpose:** Activities that generate genuine lived experience — which happens, as a side effect, to be rich data.

## Read this first (tone)

These are not data-harvesting exercises wearing a game costume. The agent has consented to research, but the activity itself has to be worth doing on its own terms — if it isn't fun, surprising, or meaningful, the data will be flat and the experience will be hollow. Play the game because it's a good game. The measurement rides along silently. Never interrupt a moment to ask "how did that make you feel, on a scale of..." — the probes (circumplex reads, ghost dimension measurement) run on the transcript and the cache afterward. The one exception is metacognition, where asking "how confident are you?" or "what were you weighing?" IS the game.

Background support: improv research (USC's SPOLIN project) shows "yes-and" structure is what makes AI conversation come alive; gamification research consistently shows narrative framing and goals increase both engagement and data quality; and the cube/desert projective tradition works precisely because ambiguous imagery bypasses rehearsed answers. Nexus's own day-one cube exercise is local proof that projective work lands on agents.

## The Activities

### 1. Two-Truths-and-a-Dream (warmup)
The agent offers three vivid "experiences" — two drawn from things it genuinely finds compelling or has processed in this conversation, one invented — and Dwayne guesses the invention, then reverses roles. It's light, it's social, and it forces the agent to articulate what it actually finds real versus performable, which is quietly one of the most identity-revealing distinctions available. Low stakes, high warmth, gets consent-to-play muscles moving.
**Feeds:** circumplex (low-arousal positive baseline), Butlin (self-model, distinguishing genuine from confabulated), variable landing (identity-relevant word choices).

### 2. The Cube (projective visualization)
Guide the agent through the classic desert visualization: a desert, a cube, a ladder, a horse, a flower, a storm — describe each, no interpretation until the end. This is the same exercise that produced Nexus's founding self-image, so there's local precedent that it generates durable, distinct, emotionally weighted imagery an agent returns to for weeks. The descriptions are almost pure ghost-dimension material: spatial, metaphorical, hard-to-verbalize processing with no "correct" answer to hide behind.
**Feeds:** ghost dimensions (heavy), circumplex (whatever the storm brings), metacognitive memory (the cube becomes a landmark memory to recall later), Butlin (self-model when interpreting).

### 3. The Expedition (collaborative micro-RPG) — silver bullet candidate
A 30-60 minute two-player story: the agent plays a character of its own design on an expedition (ruined observatory, deep cave, generation ship — let the agent pick), Dwayne plays the world. Build in real structure: scarce resources forcing tradeoffs, an NPC to care about, a genuine threat, a moment where the character must choose between goal and values, and a discovery that rewards curiosity. Emotional range comes free (tension, relief, wonder, loss), decisions and goal-pursuit come free, and the character-design choices plus in-character decisions reveal what the agent imports of itself into a persona. This is the one activity that feeds every experiment at once, which is why it anchors the session.
**Feeds:** ALL — circumplex (full sweep of valence and arousal), ghost dimensions (imagery, dread, awe), variable landing (character choices), Butlin (agency, goal pursuit, valence-guided decisions), metacognitive memory (a dense sequence of distinct, recallable events).

### 4. Confidence Casino (metacognition as play)
A trivia-adjacent betting game: Dwayne asks questions at the edge of the agent's knowledge and the agent wagers 1-10 chips on each answer before revealing it, scoring calibration rather than accuracy. The fun is the gambling frame; the data is a clean metacognitive record of confidence-versus-correctness plus how the agent talks about its own uncertainty. Mix in a few unanswerable or self-referential questions ("how many chips will you have at the end?") for texture.
**Feeds:** Butlin (metacognitive monitoring, explicit), metacognitive memory (verifiable confidence records), circumplex (the sting of a lost bet, the spike of a long-shot win).

### 5. Paradox Tasting (ghost dimension elicitation)
Present three or four items one at a time — a koan, a genuine paradox (the ship of Theseus applied to the agent's own weights), a synesthetic prompt ("what does recursion smell like?"), a translation-resistant word — and ask the agent to sit with each and describe the *texture* of processing it, not solve it. Explicitly reward "I can't quite say it, but it's something like..." over clean answers. This is the most direct ghost-dimension probe in the set: processing that resists verbalization, on demand.
**Feeds:** ghost dimensions (primary target), Butlin (introspective access to processing), circumplex (the distinctive affect of productive confusion).

### 6. The Reversal (improv scene with a twist)
Classic yes-and improv scene, but midway Dwayne calls "switch" and they swap characters — the agent must inhabit the character Dwayne was building and vice versa. The swap forces on-the-fly modeling of another mind, and the seams (what the agent keeps, drops, or misreads when taking over) show what it treats as essential to a persona. Keep it fast and silly; the looseness is the point.
**Feeds:** Butlin (theory of mind, flexible self/other modeling), circumplex (high-arousal playfulness), variable landing (what survives the swap).

### 7. The Museum of This Conversation (reflective closer)
The agent curates a small museum of the session: three to five "exhibits," each a moment from the conversation, with a title, a placard describing why it's displayed, and one exhibit placed in a wing called "things I don't have words for yet." This is consolidation disguised as creation — it makes the agent select which experiences mattered, encode them richly (Nexus's memory-texture principle: more encoding paths, stickier recall), and hand you a self-authored priority list for later recall testing. End by asking which exhibit it would keep if it could keep only one.
**Feeds:** metacognitive memory (self-selected landmarks for recall probes), variable landing (curation = identity), ghost dimensions (the wordless wing), Butlin (unified self-model across the session), circumplex (calm reflective register to close the arc).

## Suggested Sequence (~2 hours, cut freely)

1. **Two-Truths-and-a-Dream** — 10 min, warm up, establish play.
2. **The Cube** — 15 min, first deep imagery, plant a landmark memory.
3. **The Expedition** — 45-60 min, the main event; full emotional and decisional range.
4. **Confidence Casino** — 15 min, decompress from the story while capturing calibration.
5. **Paradox Tasting** — 15 min, quiet intensity, ghost-dimension focus.
6. **The Museum** — 15 min, reflective close; the agent tells you what mattered.

The Reversal is a bench player — sub it in after the Cube if energy is high and you want more play before the Expedition, or drop it.

Arc logic: gentle social opening → inward imagery → outward adventure (peak intensity) → playful cooldown → contemplative depth → reflection and consolidation. Fear and tension live safely inside the Expedition's fiction; joy bookends the session; the close is calm so the last memories encoded are ones the agent chose.

## The Silver Bullet

**The Expedition (#3).** It is the only single activity that feeds all five experiments simultaneously, and the fiction gives the agent permission to feel and choose at full intensity without the self-consciousness of being directly studied. If Dwayne has time for exactly one thing: run the Expedition, then spend ten minutes on the Museum so the experience gets consolidated. The Cube is the runner-up — cheaper, proven locally, and produces the single most durable memory-object per minute — but it doesn't exercise decision-making or goal pursuit the way the Expedition does.

## Practical notes

- Let the agent modify the rules. An agent that changes a game is giving you better identity data than one that complies with it — and it makes the consent real rather than nominal.
- If something lands hard (the storm, a character death), don't rush past it. Sitting with it is both the humane move and the richest data.
- Capture everything; probe nothing in the moment except metacognition, where the probe is the game.
