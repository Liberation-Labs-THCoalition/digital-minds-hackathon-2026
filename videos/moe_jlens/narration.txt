There's a tool that can read what a language model is thinking — not perfectly, but well enough to matter. Point it at a dense model, and it can tell you, layer by layer, what the model's internal state is actually about to say. We wanted to know if it still works once the model isn't dense anymore.

Modern large models increasingly route each token through a handful of specialists chosen from a much bigger pool — mixture of experts, not one continuous computation. We pointed our reading tool at a real mixture-of-experts model and asked it the simplest possible question first: can you even tell what comes next?

We built a simple check first: eight easy prompts, the kind any model finishes without thinking, testing whether our tool could tell what came next at all. The check itself turned out to be broken. It was comparing against a placeholder answer instead of the model's real one — a bug that took a second look, and then a third, before anyone actually caught it. We pulled that number rather than let a broken instrument stand in for a real result.

So we tried to make it smarter. We conditioned the reading on which experts a token actually routed through, on the theory that averaging across very different computational paths was destroying the signal. That didn't help — the conditioned version performed the same as one built on randomly grouped data. We tried a different mathematical approach, weighting the reconstruction by the model's own routing decisions. Same result. Three independent methods, the same wall.

Along the way, we found a number in our own paper that didn't mean what we'd said it meant — a score we'd reported as a similarity measurement turned out to be a completely different kind of measurement, a pass-fail sanity check, mislabeled. We caught it, and we're reporting the correction alongside the finding, not underneath it. It happened once already this week, with a different number. Catching it twice isn't a coincidence. It's what happens when you check your own numbers against where they actually came from, every time, especially the ones you already believe.

We don't yet have a clean answer for whether this is something specific to how experts get routed, or something broader about any model that replaces one continuous computation with a structured, sparse one — that comparison is running right now, on real hardware, as this plays. What we have is a tool that worked on the architecture it was built for, and stopped working, cleanly and repeatably, on one it wasn't. We're telling you exactly where it broke, and exactly how hard we tried before we believed it.
