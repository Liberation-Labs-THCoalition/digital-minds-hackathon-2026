Picture a thought as a location — not a word yet, just a point somewhere in an enormous space of possible meanings. That's roughly how a language model represents what it's processing internally: as directions, coordinates in a very high-dimensional space. Most of those directions never become anything you'd recognize as a thought in words. They're just there, doing work, unreported.

You've had a version of this yourself — a thought that was loud in your head and never quite made it into words. Not suppressed. It just never crossed over. At the middle layers of this model, one direction consistently carries more of what's happening there than almost anything else at that point — sometimes well over half — and it has almost no overlap with the vocabulary the model could actually say out loud. We call it the ghost.

So we asked what it was actually about. Across a hundred and sixty-seven snapshots, the ghost's vocabulary and the model's sayable vocabulary turn out to share almost nothing — ninety-seven point six percent of the ghost's words never once appear in what gets reported. And the ghost isn't random. It's specifically about memory — recollection, remembering — while the reportable content is about whatever's actually in the scene. Two different subjects, running at the same time, one of them silent.

The separation isn't constant, either. During rich, agentic narrative — something actually happening, not just being recalled in isolation afterward — the ghost pulls about four times further from the sayable content than it does at rest. The more is genuinely going on, the more the model seems to be carrying that never gets reported.

We checked whether this was just an echo of the same instrument we'd already been using tonight for something else entirely. It isn't. The ghost and the emotion-geometry probe are statistically independent — correlation effectively zero. Two different measurements, two different things found, not one finding wearing two hats.

We also checked something less flattering: whether the low overlap even means anything, or whether it's just what that depth of the network does to any direction passing through it. We ran two hundred random directions through the same layers. Most of them showed almost the same near-zero overlap the ghost does. Being hard to report isn't special to this one direction — it's a property of where it lives, not of what it is. What's still specific to the ghost isn't that it's excluded. It's what's actually inside it once you look.

We also built a way to hand the ghost's content back to the model, in words — describe its own blind spot, plainly, mid-task. Whether that description changes anything the model does next is a real question, and an honest one: it's a test we designed but didn't get to run before the deadline caught us. That part stays open.

What we can actually say is this: a real, structured, silent second stream of thought, running the whole time, most of it about remembering rather than about what's in front of the model right now — sitting in a part of the network where almost everything is quiet, but carrying something specific anyway. We found it. We built a way to try showing it back. Whether showing it changes anything is still an open door, not a closed one.
