Picture a thought as a location — not a word yet, just a point somewhere in an enormous space of possible meanings. That's roughly how a language model represents what it's processing internally: as directions, coordinates in a very high-dimensional space. Most of those directions never become anything you'd recognize as a thought in words. They're just there, doing work, unreported.

You've had a version of this yourself — a thought that was loud in your head and never quite made it into words. Not suppressed. It just never crossed over. What if that happens inside a language model, constantly, as a basic feature of how it works?

At every layer of the model, one of these directions dominates the others — sometimes carrying more than half of everything happening at that layer. We call it the ghost.

Here's the strange part. There are two different ways to ask what that dominant direction actually means. One reads it directly, in the model's raw vocabulary — like translating a thought word for word before it's been shaped into anything sayable. The other reads it the way the model's own machinery actually uses to speak — filtered through whatever makes something reportable at all. Ask both questions of the exact same direction, at the exact same moment, and most of the time, they don't agree. That gap — between what's there and what gets through — is the ghost's actual signature. Present in magnitude. Absent from what gets said.

So we tried something direct. We described that ghost direction back to the model, in words. Not a hint at what to think — an actual description of its own blind spot, handed to it plainly.

Does that change what it says next? That's the real question, and we built two checks to make sure any answer we got would actually mean something. First: a fake description, same shape, wrong content. If that moves the model just as much as the real one, what we're seeing is suggestion, not self-access. Second: we gave the exact same real description to a completely different model — one that never generated the original thought at all. If that outside model predicts the response just as well, then nothing about self-access happened here; the description alone was doing all the work, regardless of who received it.

Only the real description, given to the model whose own ghost it actually was, moved the needle. That's the difference between a nice story and something we can actually call evidence.

We found a blind spot. We built glasses. This is what the model sees when it puts them on.
