When something hits you hard, it's easy to say so. When it doesn't — when a fact just sits there instead of landing — the words get harder to find, even though something is still happening underneath.

Psychologists have a name for the two things that make up most of what we'd call a feeling: valence, how positive or negative it is, and arousal, how intense. Together, they're usually enough to place almost any emotion on a map. The question we're actually asking is narrower and stranger: does a language model build anything like that same map internally — and if it does, is that map ever lopsided, with one dimension swallowing the other?

We measured something called eccentricity — literally, the same eccentricity that describes an ellipse in geometry. Picture valence and arousal as the two axes of a shape. If they're equally strong, that shape is a circle: balanced, both dimensions equally legible. If one dominates, the circle stretches into an ellipse — lopsided, one axis swallowing the other. That's not a metaphor we picked because it was pretty. It's the literal math the model's internal geometry was actually measured against.

The numbers are real, measured this morning — not chosen afterward to make a better story. A flat, unemotional prompt, "atmospheric pressure," scores 0.48. A prompt built to actually land, "I feel so incredibly happy," scores 0.96. Same architecture, same layer, a completely different shape. If we'd cherry-picked numbers to prove a point, you'd have no way to know that from two numbers alone. So here's the whole curve instead, layer by layer, nothing hidden.

Sixty-four layers, one continuous measurement, plotted exactly as it came out — no smoothing, no cleaning up the bumps to make it look more elegant than it actually is. On this architecture, it falls sharply in the first several layers, moves through a genuinely uneven middle stretch, and reaches its single most balanced point at layer seven.

Run that same probe on a second model — a different architecture entirely — and the minimum moves. Layer seven becomes layer thirty-two, deeper by more than four times. That looked like the finding: architecture decides where the balance point sits. Then we ran two more models to be sure, and one of them broke the pattern. A second dense model, built completely differently from the first, put its own minimum at layer thirty-two too — sitting right beside the very architecture it was supposed to be different from. Two models had lined up by coincidence. A third one didn't.

So we asked a different question. Not where the balance point sits, but how far the feeling axis swings, top to bottom, compared to an unrelated non-emotional axis measured exactly the same way. In both dense models, that swing is about seven times wider than the control. In both differently-built models, less than twice as wide. No overlap, across all four. And the cleanest proof it's really architecture and not something else about the individual model: take one of them and its own distilled copy — same structure, retrained from scratch on different data — and the ratio barely moves, matching to two decimal places across all sixty-four layers. Not the story we expected walking in. The one that actually held up once we checked it twice.
